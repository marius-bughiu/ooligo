## What it is

QM — short for Quartermaster — is Y Combinator's internal agent harness, open-sourced under the MIT license on 31 July 2026. YC runs it across its own accounting, legal, events, and engineering work, and the public repo at `github.com/yc-software/qm` passed 15,000 stars within two months. The launch post on Hacker News reached 682 points and 163 comments, with most of the thread arguing QM against [OpenClaw](/en/tools/openclaw/), Hermes, and [Claude Cowork](/en/tools/claude-cowork/).

The design choice that separates it from those tools is **multiplayer**. OpenClaw is one person's agent on one person's Gateway. QM gives every employee a private agent workspace *and* gives every Slack channel, group DM, and project its own shared agent. Each of those scopes owns its memory, files, keychain view, permissions, crons, web apps, and a durable sandbox, and resources stay in the owning scope unless someone explicitly shares them. An organization scope above them sets defaults and policy ceilings.

It is not a SaaS product. You run it in your own cloud account, with your own model keys. `qm init` scaffolds a deployment repo targeting Fly.io or AWS (ECS Fargate plus Lambda MicroVMs); the core is TypeScript on Fastify with Postgres for sessions, memory, and the durable queue. The agent loop itself is pluggable — Pi, OpenCode, Codex, and [Claude Code](/en/tools/claude/) can each drive the same core — so switching model vendors is a config change, not a migration.

## Why it lands on shortlists

- **Shared agents without a shared bot token.** The usual way a team "shares" an agent is one Slack bot with one set of credentials that everyone can talk to. QM maps Slack identity to QM principals, so the agent in `#legal-intake` acts with that channel's grants and the agent in your DMs acts with yours. For a legal or finance team that is the difference between a pilot security can approve and one it can't.
- **Background work is first-class.** Crons re-run a prompt on a calendar or polling schedule, process watches report long-running jobs back into a conversation, and inbound webhooks let an external system wake a turn with a verified event. That covers the ops patterns that matter — a weekly pipeline-hygiene sweep, a contract-renewal watch, an ATS webhook that triggers candidate summaries — without a separate automation tool.
- **Skills and apps are shareable assets.** Skills are scope-owned instruction packages, shareable by grant and promotable org-wide, importable from git. Apps are internal web apps with a stable URL, immutable versions, access grants, and rollback — the agent can build the intake form and publish it to the people who need it.
- **Approval posture is an admin setting.** Strict pauses every tool call for human approval; Auto (the default) blocks private-network access and screens content; Dangerous removes gates. Command-policy rules apply as hard denials in every posture, and agent actions are audit-logged. MCP servers register as shared connectors, and QM applies its own scope rules before exposing their tools.

## What it costs

The software is $0. There is no published hosting estimate, and anyone quoting a monthly figure is guessing about your workload. The bill has four lines: cloud compute and managed Postgres on Fly.io or AWS; the sandbox backend (E2B, Modal, Sprites, and Smolmachines are supported); model tokens, billed directly by your provider; and — the line people leave out — the platform engineer who owns upgrades, identity, backups, and access reviews. Release v0.1.13 (27 September 2026) added an admin spend dashboard and per-cron runtime overrides, so token spend is at least visible from inside the product.

For contrast on the managed side: [Dust](/en/tools/dust/) Pro seats are €24 per month billed yearly, [Lindy](/en/tools/lindy/) Plus is $29.99 per user per month, and a Claude Team standard seat is $25 per month. At 20 seats that is roughly $500-$600 a month before overages. QM wins on cash only once those seat lines exceed your hosting bill plus a meaningful slice of an engineer's time.

## Best for

- Startups of roughly 10-500 people with at least one platform engineer, already Slack-first, that want ops, legal, and finance staff on shared agents without sending source systems to a third-party agent cloud
- RevOps and legal-ops teams that need channel-scoped agents — a deal-desk channel agent, a contract-intake agent — with crons and webhooks attached
- Teams already running OpenClaw for individuals who have hit its one-trust-boundary-per-Gateway limit

## Alternatives — and when to pick them instead

- **[OpenClaw](/en/tools/openclaw/)** — pick it when the agent is for one person. One daemon on a laptop or small VPS is far less to operate than QM's cloud deployment, and its own docs say a shared team Gateway is outside the design anyway.
- **[Dust](/en/tools/dust/)** — pick it when procurement needs a vendor to countersign. SOC 2 Type II, SSO, US/EU data residency, and a DPA on Enterprise answer questions QM hands back to you.
- **[Claude Cowork](/en/tools/claude-cowork/)** and [ChatGPT](/en/tools/chatgpt/) business plans — the assistants most companies already license, and the market-share leaders QM is measured against. Pick them when you are standardised on one model lab and nobody will own infrastructure.
- **[Lindy](/en/tools/lindy/)** — pick it when the builders are non-engineers and a pooled credit model is easier to budget than a cloud bill.
- QM is itself the fastest-growing entrant here, at 15,000 stars in under two months — a reason to pilot it, and a reason to pin a release.

## Watch-outs

- **The project calls itself early, experimental software.** SECURITY.md says its design goal is "not a promise that data cannot leak" and not a substitute for a deployment-specific review; releases are still in the 0.1.x range and shipping weekly. Guard: pin the `@yc-software/qm` version in your deployment repo, use the `plan` command before every upgrade, and run your own security review before regulated data moves.
- **It does not protect you from your own operator or admins.** The threat model assumes one organization of authenticated internal users; admins can read sensitive scope content (audited, not consent-gated), and sandbox credentials sit in plaintext while in use. Guard: keep the operator role to one or two named people, put privileged legal matters in a separate deployment, and review the admin audit log monthly.
- **Command policy is a speed bump, not a wall.** The project says so directly, and browser actions bypass the core gates and egress proxy. Guard: start new scopes in Strict posture with read-only or draft-only skills, and move to Auto only after a few weeks of reviewed runs.
- **No vendor means no SLA.** YC Software maintains it with no published support commitment, and contributions are requested as written proposals rather than pull requests. Guard: budget a named owner with standing time for upgrades, and if you can't name that person, buy Dust or Cowork instead.
