## What it is

Crosby is a law firm, not a software product. It reviews and negotiates commercial contracts — NDAs, MSAs, DPAs and procurement agreements — with AI agents doing the first pass and licensed attorneys signing off on what goes back to the client. The legal work is delivered by Crosby Legal PLLC; the agents are built by Crosby Legal, Inc. The firm states it carries full malpractice insurance, and its Series B announcement says "we stand behind our work and take liability for it." No review tool on the market makes that promise.

Ryan Daniels (CEO, formerly at Cooley and a startup general counsel) and John Sarihan (CTO, an early Ramp engineer) founded it in New York in September 2024, soft-launched in January 2025 and went public in June 2025. It has raised about $85.8M: a $5.8M seed led by Sequoia, a $20M Series A in October 2025 with Index, Bain Capital Ventures and Elad Gil, and a $60M Series B on 31 March 2026 led by Lux and Index, at a reported $400M valuation. The site now claims 50+ licensed attorneys. Named clients include Cursor, Clay, Cognition and Tishman Speyer; Forbes reported Cursor had sent about 2,000 contracts through Crosby by March 2026. On 29 October 2026 Crosby filed a letter of intent for the brand and some internal data of the winding-down firm Weil, Gotshal & Manges; terms were not disclosed and no outcome has been reported.

## Why it shows up in Legal Ops stacks

- **It replaces the work, not the lawyer's tooling.** You send a contract through Slack or email; it comes back reviewed against a playbook Crosby keeps for your company, with redlines and the negotiation position. Nobody on your side has to learn a Word add-in, which is the whole point for a company with no lawyer to drive one.
- **Turnaround is measured in hours.** Crosby reported a 58-minute median review time at launch in June 2025, and in October 2025 said it still returns over 90% of documents within a few hours. That is the bar outside counsel usually misses on a Friday-afternoon order form.
- **The price is per outcome.** One fee per contract by document type, not hourly billing, and third-party coverage reports one charge covers repeated review rounds on the same agreement.

## Pricing reality

Crosby publishes no price list; "Learn about pricing" on its site leads to a sales form. Forbes reported $250–1,000 per contract in March 2026, roughly $10–50 per page. Treat the bottom of that band as an NDA price and the top as a negotiated MSA or DPA.

The math decides the purchase. At a $400 midpoint, 15 contracts a month costs about $72,000 a year. A [GC AI](/en/tools/gc-ai/) seat is published at $500 a month, about $6,000 a year — the same as roughly one Crosby MSA per month. Software wins as soon as you have a lawyer who will use it; Crosby wins when the alternative is a founder, a sales leader or a lone GC reviewing paper themselves, or outside counsel at hourly rates. Axiom reports a 2026 average of $152 an hour for its commercial-contracts work, so a three-hour MSA review through an ALSP lands mid-band at about $456 before any second round.

## Best for

A COO, head of sales ops or solo general counsel at a venture-backed US company doing roughly 10–60 inbound customer and vendor contracts a month on standard templates, where deal speed matters and nobody in-house has time to review. That is the Cursor and Clay profile, and it is where Crosby's per-contract fee undercuts both a new legal hire and outside counsel.

Skip it if you already have three or more commercial lawyers in-house: at a few hundred contracts a month, the per-document bill runs into six figures a year and a review tool your team drives is far cheaper. Skip it too for litigation, M&A, regulatory work or bespoke deals — Crosby sells commercial contract review, and nothing on its site covers anything else.

## Versus the alternatives

On the "buy a tool and keep the work in-house" side, [Spellbook](/en/tools/spellbook/) is the volume leader in Word-native contract AI; pick it when a lawyer drafts and redlines all day. [LegalOn](/en/tools/legalon/) is the playbook-driven review platform built for in-house teams; pick it when a legal team of three or more wants attorney-written playbooks applied consistently. [Ivo](/en/tools/ivo/) competes for the same in-house review buyer, and [GC AI](/en/tools/gc-ai/) is the self-serve route with a published price for a single in-house lawyer.

On the "buy the labor" side, Axiom is the established ALSP; pick it when you need a seconded lawyer embedded in your team rather than document-by-document review. The fastest-growing comparable model is [Eudia](/en/tools/eudia/), which pairs software with acquired ALSPs and its own law firm, Eudia Counsel, priced as a fixed annual fee — pick it at Fortune 2000 scale when you want to convert an entire outside-counsel budget, not a contract queue. Pick Crosby when volume is moderate, templates are standard, and nobody in-house can own review. If none fit, keep outside counsel for bespoke paper and put an intake tool such as [Checkbox](/en/tools/checkbox/) or [Sandstone](/en/tools/sandstone/) in front of it so only the contracts that need a lawyer reach one.

## Watch-outs

- **Per-contract pricing scales linearly with deal volume.** A sales team that doubles its pipeline doubles the legal bill. Guard: get the rate card by document type in writing, model next year's contract volume, and set the threshold — a review-tool seat costs about as much as one or two Crosby contracts a month — at which a hire plus a review tool becomes cheaper.
- **Your playbook lives with Crosby.** Positions are captured in Crosby's per-client memory, not in a document you own. Guard: write your fallback positions down yourself, and require in the retainer that Crosby exports the playbook and the redline history of every agreement on request.
- **Admissions and data terms are not on the public site.** Crosby names no bar admissions, its readable pages state no SOC 2 attestation or model-training policy (the trust center needs access), and its terms-of-service page is still a September 2024 template saying the service "does not provide legal advice." Guard: before sending customer DPAs, get the retainer naming the responsible attorneys and covered governing law, the malpractice limits, and the security pack, including whether client contracts train the agents.
