[Filevine](/en/tools/filevine/) and [Clio](/en/tools/clio/) both sell software that runs a law firm, and the shortlist that puts them side by side is almost always a plaintiff or litigation practice deciding whether to stay on general practice management or move to something built for case workup.

The axis most buyer guides use — customization against speed of setup — is real but secondary. The one that decides the bill is this: Clio charges for the people, Filevine charges for the work. Clio is a per-seat subscription. Filevine is a seat price with a meter grid on top, and the meters count projects, depositions, tasks, and client portal invitations. A twelve-person personal injury firm carrying 2,000 open files and a twelve-lawyer general practice carrying 300 matters pay roughly the same to Clio and very different amounts to Filevine.

## What changed in 2026

Both vendors bought their way into legal research from opposite ends, so "it connects to your research tool" no longer separates them.

Clio closed a $1B acquisition of vLex alongside a $500M Series G at a $5B valuation and shipped Clio Work, a research and drafting product running on Clio Library — over 1 billion documents from 100+ jurisdictions, with a citator called Cert. Filevine raised $400M led by Insight Partners at a roughly $3B valuation in September 2025, acquired Parrot (now Depositions by Filevine, carrying the MedChron medical-chronology tool) and Pincites (now LOIS for Word), then shipped LOIS Console on 2 June 2026 and LOIS Legal Research on 22 June 2026.

The two research products are different shapes. Clio Library is a corpus you search. LOIS Legal Research is a passage-level citator: it checks whether one proposition you selected inside an opinion is still good law across millions of US federal and state precedential opinions, pairing citation-graph analysis with semantic retrieval so it can surface a case that undercuts the proposition without citing the original. If the job is finding authority, Clio's corpus is the larger asset. If the job is confirming that a sentence already in your brief still holds, Filevine answers the narrower question more directly.

## Where Filevine wins

- **Medical records are a first-class object.** MedChron turns record dumps into treatment chronologies, which is the single largest labor line in a PI file. It ships with LOIS Expert rather than as a separate integration.
- **Deposition work is native.** Depo CoPilot records and works up depositions inside the matter, and Filevine sells deposition services alongside the software — a workflow Clio has no answer for.
- **LOIS Console acts instead of answering.** Launched 2 June 2026, it writes back to the system of record: setting tasks, moving deadlines, updating calendars, generating documents, refreshing contacts, running reports. Clio's in-plan AI answers questions about a matter; it does not move your deadline chain.
- **Intake is a licensed product, not a form.** Lead Docket handles lead routing and conversion with 100 LeadsAI summarizations and 100 automation actions per user per month, plus texting numbers at $20 per month.
- **Reporting is built for matter volume.** Periscope ships as its own subscription — 15 raw datasets with 10 user licenses at the Enterprise tier, 3 at Small Business.

## Where Clio wins

- **You can price the entry tier without a call.** Starter is $49 per user per month, published. Filevine publishes no price anywhere on its site.
- **Accounting is in the subscription from the floor tier up.** Bank sync, receivables, and trust and operating reconciliation come with Starter rather than as a module.
- **It fits a mixed practice.** Filevine's product DNA is plaintiff-side. A firm running PI alongside family, estate, and business work gets one system in Clio instead of one system plus exceptions.
- **Nobody has to administer it.** Filevine's configurability is the reason firms on it end up with a dedicated system administrator. That is a salary, and it belongs in the comparison.
- **Court rules are priced per firm.** Additional jurisdictions run $10 each per firm per month, or $120 a year — a per-firm line rather than a per-seat one.

## Pricing reality

Neither vendor lets you build a quote from the website. They fail differently, and the difference is what you should plan around.

Clio publishes exactly one number: $49 per user per month for Starter. Core, Signature, and Elite are all "get pricing" or "book a demo," and Clio's own FAQ places AI on Core and above — so the single tier you can price yourself is the one tier without AI in it. Clio Work is demo-gated too.

Filevine publishes no prices at all, but it does publish its meters, which is more useful for modelling a bill than a headline seat rate. From Filevine's product specifications:

- Base-platform AI is metered at three chats per user per month, after which access is restricted until the next cycle.
- LOIS Assistant, Advanced, and Expert are priced per Project against a contracted annual minimum on a use-it-or-lose-it basis. Unused projects do not roll forward.
- LOIS Legal Research is per user per month and is not included in any base subscription.
- Depo CoPilot is an annual per-deposition tier. A recording of 10 minutes or more counts as one deposition, and each deposition is confined to a single day — so a two-day deposition counts as two.
- Automation and LOIS Workflows are sold in annual Task tiers, with overage fees above the tier.
- The client portal is licensed per active client invitation, counted as a unique email and case-number pair.

Model both against case volume, not headcount. Ten Clio Starter seats is $5,880 a year and you can confirm that today. Ten Filevine seats is unknowable from the website, and the seat line is not where the money is.

## The trap to avoid

Filevine's trap is the project meter meeting plaintiff-practice reality. A Project counts as active until it is archived, and PI files stay open for years behind liens, fee disputes, and minors' settlements. A firm that sizes its annual minimum on new matters per year and then never archives drifts into overage against a definition it agreed to before it understood. The guard: count non-archived matters older than 24 months in your current system before signing, write an archiving policy, and size the minimum against a closed-file rate you can hold. Use-it-or-lose-it cuts both directions — over-buying is not refunded either.

Clio's trap is reading the $49 headline as the price of the thing you are evaluating. Starter has no AI, and the capabilities that make Clio competitive against Filevine — Clio Work, and Clio Grow at Elite — sit behind quotes. The guard: request Core, Signature, Elite, and Clio Work pricing in the first week, before a trial has absorbed your evaluation time.

## Verdict

- **Pick Filevine** when case workup is the work — medical-record-heavy PI, mass tort, or class action, where chronologies, demand packages, and depositions are the product; when matter volume runs into the thousands; and when someone will own configuration full-time.
- **Pick Clio** when the practice is mixed or non-contingency; when the firm is under about fifty lawyers and wants billing, trust accounting, and accounting in one subscription instead of a module list; when nobody will administer the system; or when you have to size a budget this quarter.
- **Pick neither** when the firm is defense-side and time-and-billing at scale is the real constraint, where Aderant or Elite 3E is the category; or when the buyer is an in-house legal team, since both products put trust accounting and firm billing at the center. If published numbers on every tier matter more than capability, [MyCase](/en/tools/mycase/) prices all three of its plans — see [Clio vs MyCase](/en/vs/clio-vs-mycase/).

Default when you cannot separate those conditions: Clio. Not because it beats Filevine on capability for a plaintiff shop — it does not — but because you can start at a known price while the Filevine decision is a case-volume model you should build with real archived-matter counts rather than settle from a shortlist. Move to Filevine when the trial shows the constraint is chronologies, demands, and depositions rather than [matters](/en/learn/matter-management/) and invoices.
