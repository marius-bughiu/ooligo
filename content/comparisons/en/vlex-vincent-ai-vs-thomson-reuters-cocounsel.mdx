[vLex Vincent AI](/en/tools/vlex-vincent-ai/) and [Thomson Reuters CoCounsel](/en/tools/thomson-reuters-cocounsel/) both sell the same sentence: ask a legal question, get an answer with citations you can check. Both now ship inside something larger — Vincent has run under Clio since Clio closed its US$1 billion acquisition of vLex on 10 November 2025, and CoCounsel is the AI layer over Westlaw and Practical Law. The routing question is not which model reasons better. It is whether your work crosses jurisdictions and already lives in a practice-management system, or whether it is US-centric, litigation- and transaction-heavy, and grounded in a Westlaw contract you renew anyway.

## Where Vincent wins

**Jurisdictional reach inside one subscription.** Vincent answers from the Clio Library, which Clio describes as over 1 billion legal documents from more than 100 countries, sourced from courts and publishers rather than scraped from the web, with historical versions of statutes and regulations so you cite the law as it stood at the time. Citations run through Cert, Clio's citator, to confirm the authority is still good law. Westlaw's depth is US-first, and Thomson Reuters says the rebuilt CoCounsel Legal reaches Canada, the UK, and Australia only after the US release. When a matter crosses a border, CoCounsel's answer stops at the edge of its corpus and the firm buys a second research contract; Vincent's 50-State Survey and Compare Jurisdictions workflows are built for exactly that scope.

**The matter is already in the system.** Clio Manage holds the firm's matters, contacts, documents, and billing, and Vincent reads from it directly instead of asking the lawyer to assemble a document set first. CoCounsel added matter Workspaces in its June 2026 rebuild, but there is no practice-management system underneath — the documents arrive by upload or through a DMS connector (Microsoft 365, SharePoint, iManage, NetDocuments). For a 4-attorney firm that already runs on Clio, that difference removes a step from every research task, every day.

**Litigation intelligence sold as part of the seat.** Vincent ships Profile a Judge, Profile a Lawyer, Profile a Law Firm, and Profile a Party as standard workflows, and Clio Docket adds real-time alerts when a filing, motion, or order lands on one of your cases plus search across roughly 1 billion US court filings — federal district, appellate, and bankruptcy, and also PTAB, TTAB, ITC, and NLRB proceedings. Thomson Reuters sells its analytics and docket coverage as separately priced Westlaw modules.

**Cost per research seat.** vLex publishes no rate card, so this is third-party reporting rather than a vendor figure: self-serve access with Vincent included is quoted around $65–69/user/month, with firm plans nearer $399/user/month. More than 40 state bars bundle the vLex Fastcase research base as a member benefit, which turns the buying decision into the AI line alone.

## Where CoCounsel wins

**Independently benchmarked document work.** The Vals Legal AI Report (published 27 February 2025) is the only public benchmark both products entered. On the three tasks they both opted into, CoCounsel scored higher on all three: data extraction 73.2% vs Vincent's 69.2%, document Q&A 89.6% vs 72.7%, and document summarization 77.2% vs 58.9%, against a lawyer control group at 71.1%, 70.1%, and 50.3%. Vincent also entered redlining at 53.6% against a 79.7% lawyer baseline — the one task in the study where a tool scored well below the humans it was meant to relieve. Treat the whole benchmark as dated: it predates Vincent Studio, the Clio integration, and CoCounsel's own June 2026 rebuild, so read it as evidence about document-task engineering rather than a current scoreboard.

**Practical Law has no counterpart on the Vincent side.** CoCounsel reasons over Westlaw primary law *and* Practical Law — standard documents, checklists, and practice notes maintained by more than 650 attorney-editors — with KeyCite treatment attached to the citations. For transactional lawyers checking what market-standard actually looks like, that content layer is the product. The Clio Library is deep in primary law and commentary across jurisdictions; it does not ship a maintained playbook of US deal documents.

**Agentic depth on the research task itself.** CoCounsel's Deep Research (August 2025) plans a multi-step research path, runs it across Westlaw and Practical Law, and returns a structured memo with a visible chain of reasoning. The June 2026 rebuild on Anthropic's Claude Agent SDK added Workspaces and Brief Builder, an agentic drafting surface for briefs and motions with citation checking and issue spotting attached. Thomson Reuters opened early access on 22 June 2026 with general availability planned for August 2026 in the US.

**A vendor-shipped MCP path.** Thomson Reuters and Anthropic announced an MCP integration on 12 May 2026 that lets a lawyer working in Claude hand tasks to CoCounsel Legal and route CoCounsel work back through Claude. Clio and vLex ship no official MCP server for Vincent; the Clio connectors listed in MCP directories are third-party open-source projects, which is a different security review and a different support story. If the firm already runs matter work through Claude, this is a one-line difference with real consequences.

## Pricing reality

Neither vendor publishes a price for the AI line, but they withhold it differently, and the difference is decision-relevant.

Checked 16 August 2026: Clio's pricing page publishes exactly one number — "Starting at $49/user" for the Clio Manage Starter tier — and routes Core, Signature, and Elite to "Get pricing." The Clio Work page, which is the Vincent-powered tier, publishes no figure at all; every path ends at "Book a demo." Thomson Reuters' CoCounsel Legal page also says contact sales, but Thomson Reuters runs a self-serve configurator that returns a real, bookable price online for law firms with 1 to 10 attorneys, over 1-, 2-, and 3-year terms; firms above 10 attorneys are told to call 888-728-7677. So the expensive product is the one you can price without talking to a salesperson, and the cheap one is the one you cannot price at all.

On third-party reads of that configurator from June 2026, Westlaw Advantage with CoCounsel Essentials lands near $639/user/month for a solo attorney on all-states-and-federal coverage, with the research product alone reported around $256.75/month for a single circuit and $399.75/month for all states and federal. Set against the reported Vincent numbers, that is roughly 9–10× at the self-serve end and about 1.6× against Vincent firm plans. Both sets of figures are estimates and neither is vendor-published; make each vendor break out the research line and the AI line before you compare them.

The multiplier only means something if the scope matches, and it does not. A $65 Vincent seat does not include Practical Law or KeyCite. A $639 CoCounsel seat does not include non-US primary law or a practice-management system of record. The honest framing is that these are different bundles at different prices, not the same bundle at a 10× spread.

## Implementation effort

Vincent is a tier change for a firm already on Clio and a platform commitment for one that is not — buying Vincent outside Clio means buying into Clio's direction for the system of record, not just an AI seat. Clio for Word, the Word add-in that runs Vincent against the open document and returns edits as tracked changes, has been in beta since May 2026. Vincent Studio, the no-code workflow builder announced in January 2026, is restricted to large firms and enterprise legal teams, so a 6-attorney firm should not price it into the decision.

CoCounsel under 10 attorneys is a self-serve purchase measured in days. Above 10 attorneys it is a quoted enterprise contract with the procurement cycle that implies, and the rebuilt experience is still rolling out — a firm outside the US buying today buys the older surface and waits for the Canada, UK, and Australia releases.

## Verdict

**Pick Vincent** when the work crosses jurisdictions, the firm runs on Clio or is willing to move there, the practice is litigation-driven and lives on judge, party, and docket intelligence, or the firm is under 10 attorneys and cost per seat is the binding constraint. The failure mode of picking it wrongly: a US-only transactional practice pays for 100-country coverage it never opens and loses the deal-document playbook it uses weekly.

**Pick CoCounsel** when the practice is US-centric, Westlaw is already on the renewal list, transactional work depends on Practical Law and KeyCite, document review and summarization volume is the actual workload, or the firm routes matter work through Claude and wants a vendor-supported MCP path. The failure mode of picking it wrongly: a small firm with cross-border matters pays a Westlaw-scale bill and still buys a second research contract the first time a matter leaves the US.

**Pick neither** when the constraint is a Lexis contract you are not leaving — in that case the AI layer is Protégé, and [Protégé versus CoCounsel](/en/vs/lexisnexis-protege-vs-thomson-reuters-cocounsel/) is the comparison to read — or when the real need is drafting and negotiating transactional documents rather than researching law, where a drafting-first tool belongs in the consideration set instead. [Best legal AI research tools](/en/vs/best-legal-ai-research-tools/) covers the wider field.

**If you are choosing in a vacuum with none of those conditions binding, start with Vincent.** Not because it wins on capability — the benchmark evidence points the other way — but because the decision is reversible at $65–69/user/month with no procurement cycle, while the CoCounsel side commits a small firm to a multi-year Westlaw term. Run a month of real matters through Vincent, and if the gaps you hit are Practical Law and KeyCite rather than jurisdictional coverage, you have learned the thing that justifies the 10× and you can switch with evidence.
