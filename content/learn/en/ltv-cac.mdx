LTV:CAC divides the gross profit you expect from a customer over their lifetime by the cost of acquiring that customer. A result of 2.5× means an estimated USD 2.50 of lifetime gross profit for every dollar spent acquiring the account. It says nothing about which month that dollar comes back or how much cash is in the bank.

Take the perspective of a team deciding whether to repeat a campaign. The ten customers it won are known. Their future renewals are still an assumption. Keep those two kinds of input separate in the [downloadable cohort workbook](/downloads/revops-cohort-workbook.xlsx). Open the English sheet `Customer economics` and work in USD throughout.

## Match the spending to the customers

Suppose a sales team signs ten first-time paying customers with USD 6,250 in starting monthly recurring revenue (MRR) between them. The team attributes USD 100,000 to winning this cohort, including the acquisition effort spent on prospects that never signed. The per-customer acquisition cost (CAC) is USD 100,000 ÷ 10 = **USD 10,000**. The starting monthly revenue per account is USD 6,250 ÷ 10 = **USD 625**.

For your own export, count a customer once, at their first paid subscription. A returning customer, an upgrade by an existing account and an open opportunity each tell you something different. Give them separate labels. Include acquisition salaries, commissions and campaign costs under a documented allocation. If an account manager handles both acquisition and renewals, record how you divided the cost before comparing channels.

Spending can precede the close date. A campaign run in June can win customers in September. Attribute the relevant June costs to those wins, with a documented estimate where attribution is incomplete. Matching the customers matters more than making all dates the same. A lifetime forecast can be compared with a one-time CAC.

## Turn two assumptions into a ratio

Assume the average active customer keeps paying USD 625 each month, gross margin stays at 80%, and every active customer has a 2% chance of leaving at each month-end. The margin must include the costs of delivering the subscription. With no expansion or discounting, monthly gross profit per active customer is USD 500. The expected number of paid months under this model is 1 ÷ 0.02 = 50. Estimated LTV is USD 500 × 50 = **USD 25,000**, or **2.50× CAC**. Use the same time unit for revenue and churn. [ChartMogul’s simple LTV formula](https://chartmogul.com/saas-metrics/ltv/)

| Change one workbook input | Monthly gross profit | Estimated LTV | LTV:CAC |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Base, 80% margin and 2% monthly customer churn | USD 500 | USD 25,000 | 2.50× |
| Raise monthly customer churn to 4% | USD 500 | USD 12,500 | 1.25× |
| Restore 2% churn, reduce margin to 60% | USD 375 | USD 18,750 | 1.875× |

The second row leaves USD 2,500 in estimated lifetime gross profit per customer after acquisition, before other expenses. A ratio below 2× does not establish a loss. Likewise, a high multiple says little about the cost of acquiring the *next* ten customers. Their acquisition channel or retention pattern might differ.

## Check the estimate against renewals

For a young cohort, label the 2% rate as a scenario. Ten signups do not establish a 50-month lifetime. For mature cohorts, count distinct customers active at the start of a month and how many leave during that month. Keep customer losses separate from revenue lost through downgrades. A zero-churn observation gives no finite estimate in this formula. Use a stated horizon instead, such as gross profit actually earned through month 12, and show how many accounts have reached that age.

If losses cluster after the first renewal, chart gross profit by months since signup. Compare cohorts at the same age, and keep the younger ones visible with fewer observed months. For the budget discussion, pair the estimate with [CAC payback and existing-customer retention](/en/learn/unit-economics/). At the initial USD 500 monthly gross profit, steady-rate payback is 20 months. Departures or later payments change when the cohort actually recovers the outlay.
